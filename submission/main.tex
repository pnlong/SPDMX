\begin{abstract}
License-free music audio in large quantity remains scarce, as existing resources trade off realism, scale, and redistributability.
Public-domain symbolic music offers a path to large license-free audio corpora, but prior synthetic releases often rely on proprietary VSTs that cannot be redistributed, while naive open rendering produces acoustically weak stems.
We introduce SPDMX, a listening-guided, fully redistributable rendering of PDMX that is, to our knowledge, the largest license-free music audio dataset: $253{,}982$ songs, $490{,}159$ mono stems, and $5{,}469$ song-hours at $44.1$\,kHz, each with aligned PDMX metadata, dense MIDI, and a mixture.
$88{,}403$ songs ($34.8\%$) include two or more stems, supporting multitrack tasks such as source separation, automatic mixing, and stem-conditioned synthesis.
The full corpus also enables MIDI-to-audio and symbolic--audio modeling.
Matched-step Hybrid Demucs experiments confirm in-domain learnability and complementarity with Slakh2100.
We release SPDMX with PDMX-aligned metadata and a fully open-source rendering pipeline, so the same listening-guided recipe can be applied to other symbolic corpora when upstream scores permit redistributable audio synthesis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% PARAGRAPH 1 — NEED
% Topic sentence: Modern music models require paired mixtures and stems, but
% legally redistributable multitrack audio remains scarce.
% - Name motivating tasks: separation, accompaniment generation, automatic
%   mixing, and stem-conditioned synthesis.
% - Briefly contrast music with fields that have massive public corpora.
Massive public datasets have enabled significant progress in machine learning (ML) over the past decade, driven by the insight that simply scaling training data can yield substantial performance gains. Numerous domains have benefited from these massive corpora: text with C4~\cite{raffel2020exploring} and The Pile~\cite{gao2020pile}, images with ImageNet~\cite{deng2009imagenet} and LAION-5B~\cite{schuhmann2022laion}, and even general audio and speech with AudioSet~\cite{gemmeke2017audio} and LibriSpeech~\cite{panayotov2015librispeech}. Music cannot afford that same luxury. Despite vast quantities of music abounding on the internet, copyright licenses block public access to these potential large datasets. Meanwhile, modern music models require increasingly large quantities of mixtures, especially those paired with stems, for tasks such as source separation, accompaniment generation, automatic mixing, and stem-conditioned synthesis. Therein lies a central issue to modern AI-Music research: models require large multitrack music corpora, but legally redistributable datasets for training these systems remain scarce.

% PARAGRAPH 2 — THE EXISTING TRADE-OFF
% Topic sentence: Existing resources trade off realism, scale, and
% redistributability.
% - Recorded datasets (MUSDB18, MedleyDB, MoisesDB): realistic but small and/or
%   restricted.
% - Slakh2100: synthetic and larger, but built with non-redistributable
%   commercial instruments and still small relative to symbolic collections.
% - State the gap explicitly: no existing resource combines all three goals.
% - Place the dataset comparison table here.
While multitrack music resources exist, they trade off three key components: realism, scale, and redistributability. Recorded datasets such as MUSDB18~\cite{rafii2019musdb18}, MedleyDB~\cite{bittner2014medleydb}, and MoisesDB~\cite{pereira2023moisesdb} provide realistic, high-quality stem-separated data, but suffer from small corpora sizes and restricted access. Slakh2100~\cite{manilow2019cutting} remedies that scale issue by synthesizing abundant MIDI data as multitrack audio but does so with non-redistributable commercial instruments; furthermore, despite its impressive scale, the size of Slakh2100 still pales in comparison to existing symbolic music collections. No existing resource combines all three goals of realism, scale, and redistributability.

% PARAGRAPH 3 — OPPORTUNITY AND TECHNICAL OBSTACLE
% Topic sentence: Public-domain symbolic collections offer a path to scale, but
% naive rendering produces acoustically unconvincing stems.
% - Briefly explain PDMX's crowdsourced provenance, public-domain status,
%   contents, and scale for readers unfamiliar with it.
% - Explain why direct General MIDI rendering is insufficient: dry timbres and
%   poor sustain for winds, brass, and strings.
Symbolic music datasets, namely Public Domain MusicXML (PDMX)~\cite{long2024pdmx}, offer a path to scale and legal redistribution. PDMX collects public domain symbolic music files from MuseScore into a corpus of $\sim$254K largely classical and folk songs. While we could simply render the dataset with a General MIDI (GM) soundfont, previous work~\cite{manilow2019cutting} has shown this approach as insufficient, as GM soundfonts often suffer from dry timbres and poor sustain for winds, brass, and strings. Any such naive rendering approach would lack the realism outlined earlier as a central component of music datasets. Furthermore, while symbolic music datasets readily enable multitrack rendering, it should be noted that the open-source, license-free audio data offered by PDMX -- even at the single-track level -- has substantial utility on its own for tasks in automatic music generation, transcription, and analysis.

% PARAGRAPH 4 — SPDMX
% Topic sentence: We introduce SPDMX, a listening-guided rendering of PDMX that
% pairs public-domain scores with redistributable stems and mixtures at
% unprecedented scale.
% - Give headline corpus statistics.
% - Distinguish all rendered songs from the multitrack subset.
% - Emphasize the complete open chain: source score, MIDI, synthesizers,
%   rendering protocol, stems, mixtures, and metadata.
% - Use "to our knowledge" once and support the largest-dataset claim with the

To alleviate this issues, we introduce SPDMX, a listening-guided rendering of PDMX that pairs public-domain scores with redistributable stems and mixtures at unprecedented scale. SPDMX comprises 5{,}469 song hours, around half of which come from multitrack ($\geq 2$ stems) scores.
Our rendering chain is entirely open source: we begin by cleaning PDMX's MIDI files, after which we use listener preference to render stems with a diverse, open-source soundfont collection or MIDI-DDSP~\cite{wu2022midi} for more realistic tracks when possible. SPDMX is meant for use in conjunction with the original PDMX dataset via shared metadata. To our knowledge, SPDMX is the largest audio-domain music dataset, whose public-domain licensing allows for free, unrestricted use.

% Topic sentence: SPDMX combines a fully open synthesis protocol,

% - Contribution 1: aligned symbolic and audio corpus at public-domain scale.
% - Contribution 2: category-specific hybrid FluidSynth/MIDI-DDSP recipe.
% - Contribution 3: perceptual study used to freeze that recipe.
% - Contribution 4: matched-budget source-separation evaluation spanning

SPDMX combines a fully open synthesis protocol, listener-selected rendering strategies, and task-based evaluations. SPDMX offers three main contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Aligning a symbolic and audio corpus at scale, in the public-domain.
	\item Category-specific hybrid FluidSynth/MIDI-DDSP recipes selected through a perceptual study.
	\item Matched-budget source-separation evaluation on synthetic and recorded test domains.
\end{enumerate}
Upon acceptance, we will make SPDMX available through our demo page\footnote{\url{\demourl}}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:prior}

% PARAGRAPH 1 — RECORDED MULTITRACK DATA
% Topic sentence: Recorded multitrack datasets provide realistic supervision
% but remain too small and restricted for large-scale open training.
% - Situate MUSDB18, MedleyDB, and MoisesDB.
% - Explain what realism provides and what scale/licensing prevents.
Recorded multitrack datasets provide realistic supervision for music generation and source separation but remain too small and/or restricted for large-scale open training. MUSDB18~\cite{rafii2019musdb18}, MedleyDB~\cite{bittner2014medleydb}, and MoisesDB~\cite{pereira2023moisesdb} all comprise high-quality multitrack studio recordings, and they consistently find use in training source-separation and generative models. However, their smaller sizes (covering $\sim$10, $\sim$7.2, and $\sim$14.4 song-hours, respectively) combined with their restriction to academic research settings limits their use for large-scale training that has proven so successful in other ML domains.

% PARAGRAPH 2 — SYNTHETIC STEM DATA
% Topic sentence: Synthetic datasets increase scale, but prior large
% collections depend on proprietary sample libraries.
% - Present Slakh2100 as the closest precedent.
% - Explain what SPDMX retains: coherent stems and mixtures from symbolic music.
% - Explain what SPDMX changes: public-domain scores, open renderers, much
%   greater scale, and end-to-end redistributability.
Slakh2100~\cite{manilow2019cutting} set a precedent for using synthetic datasets derived from symbolic music---in its case, Lakh MIDI~\cite{raffel2016learning}---to increase scale. While its quality lags behind that of actual studio-recorded datasets, Slakh remains a popular choice for its large collection of $\sim$145 song-hours of multitrack music. However, Slakh's rendering process depends on proprietary sample libraries that prevent the synthesis of similar large symbolic music corpora as multitrack audio. SPDMX offers an alternative open-source paradigm for rendering coherent stems and mixtures from symbolic music: public-domain scores, open renderers, much greater scale, and end-to-end redistributability.

% PARAGRAPH 3 — NEURAL MIDI SYNTHESIS
% Topic sentence: Neural MIDI synthesis can improve realism for selected
% instruments, motivating a hybrid rather than single-renderer pipeline.
% - Discuss MIDI-DDSP, URMP, and the Chamber Ensemble Generator.
% - Set up the listening study: renderer choice is empirical and
%   instrument-dependent, not an assumption that neural synthesis always wins.
Neural MIDI synthesis offers a path to improving realism for specific instruments, which motivates a hybrid rendering pipeline instead of one entirely reliant on sample libraries. MIDI-DDSP~\cite{wu2022midi} has proven well-suited for rendering MIDI datasets as realistic audio~\cite{wu2022chamber}. However, despite strong results, its usefulness is limited to its training data, the URMP~\cite{li2018creating} dataset, which means that we can only use MIDI-DDSP for select orchestral instruments. Hence, we can render with MIDI-DDSP on instruments when it yields more realistic stems, which we can decide through a listening study, and fall back to sample libraries for the rest.

\section{SPDMX Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

% SECTION FRAMING
% Topic sentence: Constructing an open stem corpus from symbolic scores
% requires solving three problems: unreliable instrumentation metadata,
% uneven rendering quality, and mixture assembly that preserves musical
% balance.
% - Map these problems directly to the three subsections below.
We build SPDMX in three stages: clean PDMX scores, determine the most realistic renderer for each instrument category, and assemble the full release from those stems.

\subsection{Initial data cleaning}
\label{ssec:cleaning}

% PARAGRAPH 1 — WHY CLEANING IS NECESSARY
% Topic sentence: We first correct instrument metadata and remove empty tracks
% so that each rendered stem has a reliable semantic identity.
% - Explain disagreement between track names and General MIDI programs.
% - Describe alias-based program correction and removal of tracks without notes.
% - State limitations of heuristic correction if relevant.
% Topic sentence: The cleaned corpus is dominated by piano, voice/choir,
% drums, and common orchestral families, with different distributions by stem
% count and audible duration.
% - Define non-silent hours and the RMS threshold.
% - Explain why piano's share of stems exceeds its share of non-silent hours.
% - Place the corrected-program inventory figure here.
PDMX MIDI programs often disagree with track names (e.g., a named violin encoded as piano). We correct GM programs from track name aliases and drop tracks with no note events. Fig.~\ref{fig:gm_programs} shows the corrected inventory by stem count and non-silent hours: for each rendered stem, we sum $0.25$\,s windows whose RMS amplitude is at least $0.01$ ($\approx -40$\,dBFS), excluding near-silent gaps between notes and trailing padding. Much of SPDMX concentrates in piano, voice, drums, and common orchestral families. Piano dominates stem count ($57\%$) more than non-silent hours ($41\%$), indicating that many scores may include a piano track, but this track is often sparse or short.

% Topic sentence: Cleaning makes the symbolic controls reliable, but it does

Although cleaning makes the symbolic source of SPDMX more reliable, it does not resolve the acoustic limitations of using a single GM bank to render the whole dataset. Naive GM rendering remains dry and synthetic, and short soundfont notes poorly match acoustic sustain for winds, brass, and strings. That motivates listening among open FluidSynth banks and selective MIDI-DDSP.

\subsection{Listening study}
\label{ssec:listening}

% PARAGRAPH 1 — DESIGN SPACE
% Topic sentence: Because no open synthesizer performs best across all
% instrument families, we compare category-specific FluidSynth and MIDI-DDSP
% rendering strategies.
% - Define basic and varied FluidSynth conditions.
% - List representative redistributable soundfonts and the light reverb setup.
% - Define MIDI-DDSP eligibility and FluidSynth fallbacks.
% - Clarify treatment of voice tracks and exclusion of lyric synthesis.
We use a listening study to determine a render recipe for each instrument category. Every track becomes a mono stem at $44.1$\,kHz.
The \emph{basic} soundfont strategy uses one GM bank (\texttt{SGM-V2.01}). The \emph{varied} strategy samples from a shortlist of redistributable soundfonts per category, including \texttt{FluidR3\_GM}, \texttt{GeneralUser GS}, \texttt{Arachno}, and \texttt{Airfont}, plus a light reverb FX profile, for Slakh-like timbral variety without Slakh's proprietary libraries. For eligible monophonic strings, brass, and winds, MIDI-DDSP~\cite{wu2022midi} can replace the soundfont; however, we fall back to FluidSynth for polyphonic parts, instruments outside the MIDI-DDSP (URMP) inventory, and all other GM programs.
Voice-class tracks use choir or voice soundfonts (lyric singing synthesis is out of scope).

% PARAGRAPH 2 — STUDY DESIGN
% Topic sentence: Listeners rate four rendering families for perceived realism
% under shuffled, instrument-balanced comparisons.
% - Four families: basic; varied; MIDI-DDSP with basic fallback; MIDI-DDSP with
%   varied fallback.
% - State excerpt length, number of categories and stems, listener count,
%   rating scale, reference handling, and randomization.
% - Be precise about the unit of analysis and how scores are aggregated.
We ask listeners to compare four families on perceived realism: basic, varied, MIDI-DDSP over basic fallbacks, and MIDI-DDSP over varied fallbacks. The open reference is the basic soundfont, which also appears as a hidden reference among shuffled unlabeled conditions. Each trial is a $10$-second stem excerpt scored for perceived realism ($0$--$100$). For each of the 10 categories, the study has two stems. We had $N{=}8$ listeners complete the study (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation_listening}).

% PARAGRAPH 3 — DECISION RULE AND RESULT
% Topic sentence: Listener preferences support a hybrid recipe whose renderer
% depends on instrument family.
% - State the default highest-mean rule and the diversity exception.
% - Give the frozen assignment by category.
% - Place the listening-study figure here.
Based on the results of the listening study, we develop a ``recipe'' for rendering SPDMX: for each category, we pick one of the four families as winner, choosing the highest mean realism by default. When basic only narrowly beats the varied family, we keep varied for diversity. SPDMX uses basic for drums, organ, and guitar, and varied for piano, voice, mallet, and residual miscellaneous classes; strings and brass use MIDI-DDSP over basic, and wind uses MIDI-DDSP over varied ($\star$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:listening}).

\subsection{Final assembly}
\label{ssec:assembly}

% PARAGRAPH 1 — MIXING PROBLEM AND SOLUTION
% Topic sentence: We preserve MIDI velocity relationships while controlling
% loudness and clipping so that released stems remain linearly summable.
% - Explain why independent loudness normalization would erase intended balance.
% - Describe the -23 LUFS target, peak limiting, velocity-ratio scaling, and
%   one uniform anti-clipping gain on the stem sum.
% - State explicitly that each mixture equals the sum of its released stems.
The frozen recipe is applied to every cleaned song under the previously mentioned redistributable synthesizers. Because independent per-stem loudness matching would erase MIDI velocity balance, we loudness-normalize each stem toward $-23$\,LUFS~\cite{series2011algorithms} with peak limiting, after which we scale each stem by the ratio of that track's maximum note-on velocity to the song maximum. We uniformly-apply a single anti-clip peak gain on the stem sum in order to keeps stems linearly summable (mixture equals stem sum)~\cite{manilow2019cutting}.

% PARAGRAPH 2 — RELEASE CONTENTS AND SCALE
% Topic sentence: The resulting corpus provides aligned scores, MIDI, stems,
% and mixtures for 253,982 songs, including 88,403 multitrack songs.
% - Report 5,469 total song-hours, 490,159 mono FLAC stems at 44.1 kHz, and
%   2,734 multitrack song-hours.
% - Define song-hours and explain why they are not multiplied by stem count.
% - Report stem-count distribution and identify the BDGP comparison slice.
% - Explain identity-based alignment with PDMX metadata and MusicXML.
% - Name the open synthesis components and their redistribution terms.
% - Place the stems-per-song figure here.
SPDMX has $253{,}982$ songs ($5{,}469$ song-hours) and $490{,}159$ mono FLAC stems, plus paired MIDI. Song-hours are mixture durations counted once per song (not multiplied by stem count). Of these, $88{,}403$ songs ($34.8\%$; $2{,}734$ song-hours, $50.0\%$ of hours) have $\geq 2$ stems (mean $1.93$ stems per song, max $66$, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:stems_per_song}). A BDGP-eligible subset (bass, drums, guitar, and piano present) of SPDMX supports matched Slakh comparisons. We design SPDMX such that songs share identity with PDMX (e.g., MusicXML, metadata) and use only open synthesizers, keeping SPDMX open-source and license-free.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

% SECTION FRAMING
% Topic sentence: We evaluate whether SPDMX provides learnable supervision for
% source separation and whether it generalizes across synthetic and recorded
% datasets under matched training budgets.
% - State that this is a dataset validation study, not a claim of
%   state-of-the-art separation performance.

We evaluate whether SPDMX provides learnable supervision for source separation and whether it generalizes across synthetic and recorded domains. These runs validate the release rather than claim state-of-the-art separation performance. To limit confounding factors such as training duration and dataset size, we match the training budget of every training arm to the Slakh2100 four-stem protocol.

% PARAGRAPH 1 — RESEARCH QUESTION AND SETUP
% Topic sentence: We first test whether SPDMX supports in-domain source
% separation, complements Slakh across synthetic domains, and transfers to
% recorded multitrack datasets.
% - Define Hybrid Demucs and the bass/drums/guitar/piano target classes.
% - Explain SPDMX-to-class mapping and the all-four-stems eligibility filter.
% - Report training-set hours and the three matched-budget arms.
% - Define SI-SDR, aggregation, and uncertainty/repeated runs.
% - Identify the evaluation domains: held-out SPDMX, Slakh2100, MUSDB18,
%   MoisesDB, and MedleyDB.
% - Explain how each dataset's stem taxonomy is mapped to common target classes;
%   report results only for classes that can be compared fairly.
We fine-tune four-source Hybrid Demucs~\cite{defossez2021hybrid} on \emph{bass}, \emph{drums}, \emph{guitar}, and \emph{piano} (BDGP) mixtures using the Slakh2100 four-stem protocol. SPDMX stems map from General MIDI program ranges, Slakh stems from dataset metadata; we keep only songs that contain all four targets (the BDGP-eligible SPDMX pool described in \S\ref{ssec:assembly}). We train on three arms that share the same step budget on $10$\,s crops (mono duplicated to stereo): Slakh2100 alone, the SPDMX pool alone, and their union. Our primary evaluation metrics are mean scale-invariant SI-SDR~\cite{leroux2019sdr} on held-out Slakh2100 test and SPDMX validation mixtures. We additionally score recorded corpora only where a target maps cleanly: MUSDB18~\cite{rafii2019musdb18} (bass and drums), MedleyDB~\cite{bittner2014medleydb} (instrument tags), and MoisesDB~\cite{pereira2023moisesdb} (native stem names). In this way, we omit classes without a fair counterpart instead of treating missing stems as silence.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

SPDMX renders PDMX under a listening-guided open pipeline into what is, to our knowledge, the largest license-free music audio dataset: $253{,}982$ songs, $490{,}159$ mono stems, and $5{,}469$ song-hours at $44.1$\,kHz, with aligned PDMX metadata and MIDI throughout.
$88{,}403$ songs ($34.8\%$) include two or more stems for multitrack supervision.
As a whole, the release supports separation, automatic mixing, stem-conditioned generation, and MIDI-to-audio research without copyrighted recordings or proprietary sample libraries.
Matched-step separation experiments show the corpus is learnable in-domain and can be trained jointly with Slakh2100, while transfer to recorded benchmarks remains weak.
Because the render stack is fully open source and redistributable, the same recipe can be extended synthesize other symbolic corpora.
We hope SPDMX finds use as training and evaluation data for source separation, stem-conditioned generation and editing, automatic mixing, multitrack and MIDI-aligned transcription, accompaniment and arrangement models, and joint symbolic--audio research.

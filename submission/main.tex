\begin{abstract}
Large public datasets have driven recent progress in machine learning, from
text and vision to speech and general audio.
Music remains an outlier: copyright blocks redistribution of training audio,
while many modern tasks need \emph{individual stems} rather than mixtures.
Existing multitrack resources trade off realism, scale, and licensing;
synthetic alternatives rely on proprietary VSTs or naive open rendering that
yields weak stems.
We introduce \textbf{SPDMX}, a listening-guided rendering of public-domain PDMX scores
into an openly-licensed stem corpus with aligned scores and an open render
pipeline: $253{,}982$ songs, $490{,}159$ mono stems, and $5{,}469$
song-hours at $44.1$\,kHz under CC~BY~4.0, with paired MIDI and identifiers
linking to PDMX metadata.
$88{,}403$ songs ($34.8\%$; $2{,}734$ song-hours) provide multitrack
supervision. To our knowledge, this is the largest openly-licensed synthetic
multitrack subset by song-hours, enabling source separation, stem-conditioned
synthesis, MIDI-to-audio, transcription, and joint symbolic--audio modeling.
Fixed-budget experiments validate the aligned supervision for source
separation and transcription.
We release the corpus, metadata, and open pipeline for reuse on other
symbolic collections.\footnotemark[1]
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Massive public datasets have driven much of the progress in machine
learning over the past decade, from text~\cite{raffel2020exploring,gao2020pile}
and images~\cite{deng2009imagenet,schuhmann2022laion} to speech and general
audio~\cite{gemmeke2017audio,panayotov2015librispeech}.
Music cannot yet follow the same path: copyright blocks redistribution of
recordings that would form such corpora, while modern models need
\emph{individual stems} for source separation, accompaniment generation,
and stem-conditioned synthesis.
Legally redistributable multitrack supervision remains scarce.

Existing resources trade off realism, scale, and redistributability
(Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). Recorded sets such as
MUSDB18~\cite{rafii2019musdb18}, MedleyDB~\cite{bittner2014medleydb,bittner2016medleydb},
and MoisesDB~\cite{pereira2023moisesdb} are realistic but small or
restricted, while Slakh2100~\cite{manilow2019cutting} uses
non-redistributable commercial instruments. PDMX~\cite{long2024pdmx,xu2024generating}
offers symbolic scale, but naive General MIDI rendering is dry and
acoustically weak. No prior resource jointly offers score-collection scale
and an open render stack.

To address this gap, we propose SPDMX, a rendering of PDMX comprising
$253{,}982$ songs, $490{,}159$ mono stems, and $5{,}469$ song-hours at
$44.1$\,kHz under CC~BY~4.0. Its $88{,}403$ multitrack songs total
$2{,}734$ song-hours, exceeding Slakh2100's full inventory, while singles
support generation and transcription. Songs pair MIDI with PDMX identifiers.
We clean the MIDI and select category-specific
FluidSynth or MIDI-DDSP~\cite{wu2022midi} recipes through a small listening
pilot, producing a reusable open pipeline.
Fixed-budget separation and transcription experiments validate in-domain
audio--label alignment and characterize cross-domain behavior. The
release supports separation, stem-conditioned synthesis, MIDI-to-audio,
transcription, and joint symbolic--audio research. Data, samples, schema,
and code are on our project page.\footnotemark[1]

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:prior}

\noindent\textbf{Recorded multitracks.}
MUSDB18~\cite{rafii2019musdb18},
MedleyDB~\cite{bittner2014medleydb,bittner2016medleydb}, and
MoisesDB~\cite{pereira2023moisesdb} provide high-quality studio stems, while
chamber sets such as URMP~\cite{li2018creating} offer realistic ensembles at
still smaller scale.
All remain limited in hours and/or access.
SPDMX instead prioritizes open scale: thousands of song-hours of
redistributable stems with aligned scores, achieved through synthesis
rather than expensive studio recording.

\noindent\textbf{Synthetic multitracks.}
Slakh2100~\cite{manilow2019cutting} synthesizes Lakh
MIDI~\cite{raffel2016learning} into $\sim$145 song-hours of multitrack audio
but depends on proprietary sample libraries that cannot be redistributed.
Neural synthesizers such as MIDI-DDSP~\cite{wu2022midi} (trained on
URMP~\cite{li2018creating}) improve realism for selected monophonic
instruments and underlie generative releases like
CocoChorales~\cite{wu2022chamber}; EnsembleSet~\cite{sarkar2022ensembleset}
and SynthSOD~\cite{garcia2025synthsod} similarly target chamber or
orchestral stems with commercial libraries.
SPDMX instead renders \emph{real} public-domain PDMX repertoire with an open
FluidSynth/MIDI-DDSP stack, delivering stems, mixtures, and aligned
scores at a scale those resources do not jointly provide.
Among the resources surveyed in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}, its
$2{,}734$-hour multitrack subset is the largest openly-licensed synthetic
collection by song-hours.

\section{SPDMX Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

SPDMX ships $253{,}982$ songs ($5{,}469$ song-hours) as $490{,}159$ mono
FLAC stems plus paired MIDI, every file joinable to PDMX on song identity.
Song-hours are mixture durations counted once per song (not multiplied by
stem count). Of these, $88{,}403$ songs
($34.8\%$; $2{,}734$ song-hours, $50.0\%$ of hours) have $\geq 2$ stems
(mean $1.93$ stems per song, max $66$). Fig.~\ref{fig:stems_per_song}
compares multitrack song-hours by stem count against Slakh2100: even
excluding SPDMX's $165{,}579$ singles ($65.2\%$), the multitrack subset
alone already exceeds Slakh2100's full $145$\,h inventory. A 632-song
BDGP-eligible subset (bass, drums, guitar, and piano present) supports
matched Slakh2100 comparisons. Rendering uses only open synthesizers; audio
is released under CC~BY~4.0.

We build the release in three stages: clean PDMX scores, select a renderer
family for each instrument category, and assemble the full release from
those stems.

\subsection{Initial data cleaning}
\label{ssec:cleaning}

PDMX MIDI programs often disagree with track names (e.g., a named violin
encoded as piano). We correct GM programs from track name aliases and drop
tracks with no note events. Fig.~\ref{fig:gm_programs} shows the corrected
inventory by stem count and non-silent hours: for each rendered stem, we
sum $0.25$\,s windows whose RMS amplitude is at least $0.01$
($\approx -40$\,dBFS), excluding near-silent gaps between notes and
trailing padding. Much of SPDMX concentrates in piano, voice, drums, and
common orchestral families. Piano dominates stem count ($57\%$) more than
non-silent hours ($41\%$), so many piano tracks are sparse or short.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/gm_program_counts_compare.pdf}
\caption{SPDMX stem counts and non-silent hours by listening category (same
ten labels used in the rendering study). Categories marked $*$ are
MIDI-DDSP eligible in the frozen hybrid recipe (strings, wind, brass).}
\label{fig:gm_programs}
\end{figure}

Although cleaning makes the symbolic source of SPDMX more reliable, it does
not resolve the acoustic limitations of using a single GM bank to render
the whole dataset. Naive GM rendering remains dry and synthetic, and short
soundfont notes poorly match acoustic sustain for winds, brass, and
strings. That motivates listening among open FluidSynth banks and selective
MIDI-DDSP.

\subsection{Listening study}
\label{ssec:listening}

We use a small listening pilot to select a render recipe for each instrument
category. Every track becomes a mono stem stored at $44.1$\,kHz.
The \emph{basic} soundfont strategy uses one GM bank (\texttt{SGM-V2.01}).
The \emph{varied} strategy samples from a shortlist of redistributable
soundfonts per category, including \texttt{FluidR3\_GM},
\texttt{GeneralUser GS}, \texttt{Arachno}, and \texttt{Airfont}, plus a
light reverb FX profile, for Slakh2100-like timbral variety without
Slakh2100's proprietary libraries. For eligible monophonic strings, brass, and winds,
MIDI-DDSP~\cite{wu2022midi} can replace the soundfont; however, we fall
back to FluidSynth for polyphonic parts, instruments outside the MIDI-DDSP
(URMP) inventory, and all other GM programs.
MIDI-DDSP synthesizes at $16$\,kHz; we upsample to $44.1$\,kHz so all stems
share one sample rate and mix together easily, though those stems remain
band-limited relative to native $44.1$\,kHz audio.
Voice-class tracks use choir or voice soundfonts (lyric singing synthesis is
out of scope).

We ask listeners to compare four families on perceived realism: basic,
varied, MIDI-DDSP over basic fallbacks, and MIDI-DDSP over varied
fallbacks. The basic soundfont is the open reference, repeated among shuffled
unlabeled conditions. Each trial scores a $10$-second stem excerpt for
perceived realism ($0$--$100$). Two stems represent each of 10 categories.
$N{=}8$ listeners completed the study (Fig.~\ref{fig:listening}).

We treat ratings as design guidance. We choose the highest mean by default, but
when basic and varied are close, we retain varied to preserve timbral diversity. This
heuristic is not a significance rule.
SPDMX uses
basic for drums, organ, and guitar, and varied for piano, voice, mallet,
and miscellaneous classes; strings and brass use MIDI-DDSP over
basic, and wind uses MIDI-DDSP over varied ($\star$ in
Fig.~\ref{fig:listening}).

\subsection{Final assembly}
\label{ssec:assembly}

We apply the frozen recipe to every cleaned song. Because independent per-stem
loudness matching would erase MIDI velocity balance, we loudness-normalize
each stem toward $-23$\,LUFS~\cite{series2011algorithms} with peak
limiting, after which we scale each stem by the ratio of that track's
maximum note-on velocity to the song maximum. We uniformly apply a single
anti-clip peak gain on the stem sum in order to keep stems linearly
summable (mixture equals stem sum)~\cite{manilow2019cutting}.

Several limitations follow from this construction.
About $65\%$ of songs are single-stem, and piano dominates the inventory.
MIDI comes from engraved scores with quantized timing and dynamics.
All stems are mono.
The small, synthetic-reference listening study does not establish
corpus-wide absolute realism.
MIDI-DDSP stems remain band-limited after upsampling from $16$\,kHz.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We use fixed-budget source-separation and transcription experiments as two
dataset checks: whether models can recover SPDMX targets in domain, and how
strongly performance changes across synthetic and recorded domains. These
experiments validate alignment and characterize domain gaps rather than
test the benefit of scaling to the full corpus.

\subsection{Source separation}
\label{ssec:separation}

We train four-source Hybrid Demucs~\cite{defossez2021hybrid} from random
initialization on
\emph{bass}, \emph{drums}, \emph{guitar}, and \emph{piano} (BDGP) mixtures
using the Slakh2100 four-stem protocol. SPDMX stems map from General MIDI
program ranges, Slakh2100 stems from dataset metadata; we keep only songs
that contain all four targets. The 632 SPDMX songs are shuffled at the song
level into 600 training and 32 validation songs. We train three arms for
$32$K updates with batch size 8 on $10$\,s crops (mono duplicated to
stereo): Slakh2100 alone, SPDMX alone, and their concatenated union, sampled
uniformly by song. Equal updates control compute, not unique-song exposure.
We report
SI-SDR~\cite{leroux2019sdr}, which measures how closely an estimated stem
matches its reference while ignoring overall loudness. We evaluate on
Slakh2100 and SPDMX mixtures, and on recorded corpora where a target maps
cleanly: MUSDB18~\cite{rafii2019musdb18} (bass and drums),
MedleyDB~\cite{bittner2014medleydb,bittner2016medleydb} (instrument tags),
and MoisesDB~\cite{pereira2023moisesdb} (native stem names).

especially for bass and drums, but pitched-stem transfer to recordings
remains weak.

\subsection{Transcription}
\label{ssec:transcription}

Because SPDMX pairs mixtures with MIDI, we also ask whether it
supports multi-instrument automatic music
transcription~\cite{gardner2022mt3,chang2024yourmt3}. We train a
YourMT3-style encoder--decoder (T5-small, trained from scratch) for
$100$K matched steps on mixture audio with aligned MIDI, comparing
Slakh2100-redux against SPDMX songs with at least two stems. We evaluate
instrument-agnostic onset F1 on non-drum notes (pitch and onset within
$\pm 50$\,ms; offsets ignored) and Multi F1, which includes drums and
requires pitch, onset, offset, and instrument matches (drum offsets are
ignored), on the official Slakh2100 test split and a held-out SPDMX split.
This setup is
intentionally small in training budget and omits the
pretrained recipes used in published MT3-style systems, so absolute scores
remain low. We use it as an alignment and domain-gap check rather than a
comparison to published MT3-style systems (Table~\ref{tab:transcription}).

Scores are not comparable to published MT3-style systems.}

On the Slakh2100 test split, Slakh- and SPDMX-trained models reach onset F1 near $0.07$
under equal update budgets; this comparison does not isolate the effect of
additional training hours. Cross-domain Slakh$\rightarrow$SPDMX is weaker
($0.041$ onset / $0.010$ multi), indicating little free transfer into the
rendered SPDMX domain. On held-out SPDMX, the in-domain model reaches
$0.265$ onset F1, indicating usable in-domain audio--MIDI supervision.
Overall, SPDMX supports transcription training within its rendered domain,
but cross-domain transfer remains limited under this budget.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

SPDMX brings public-domain PDMX scores into the audio domain as a large
CC~BY~4.0 stem corpus: $253{,}982$ songs, $490{,}159$ mono stems, and
$5{,}469$ song-hours at $44.1$\,kHz, with paired MIDI and identifiers linking
to PDMX metadata.
$88{,}403$ songs ($34.8\%$; $2{,}734$ song-hours) supply multitrack
supervision beyond Slakh2100's full inventory; a substantial single-stem subset still
supports synthesis, transcription, and analysis. The listening-guided, open
FluidSynth/MIDI-DDSP pipeline makes the release reproducible and extensible
to other symbolic collections. Fixed-budget experiments validate usable
in-domain supervision for separation and transcription. We hope SPDMX
will support source separation, stem-conditioned synthesis, MIDI-to-audio,
transcription, and joint symbolic--audio research.

\section{Compliance with Ethical Standards}
\label{sec:ethics}

SPDMX is derived from public-domain PDMX scores; the rendered audio, MIDI,
and release tables are distributed under CC~BY~4.0.
Rendering uses soundfonts selected for redistribution-compatible terms and
open MIDI-DDSP weights (Apache~2.0; trained on URMP), not closed commercial
VST sample libraries.
The listening study involved adult volunteers rating synthetic audio excerpts.

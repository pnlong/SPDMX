\begin{abstract}
License-free music audio in large quantity remains scarce, as existing
resources trade off realism, scale, and redistributability.
Public-domain symbolic music offers a path to large license-free audio
corpora, but prior synthetic releases often rely on proprietary VSTs that
cannot be redistributed, while naive open rendering produces acoustically
weak stems.
We introduce SPDMX, a listening-guided, fully redistributable rendering of
PDMX and, to our knowledge, the largest license-free music audio dataset:
$253{,}982$ songs, $490{,}159$ mono stems, and $5{,}469$ song-hours at
$44.1$\,kHz, each with aligned PDMX metadata, MIDI, and a mixture.
$88{,}403$ songs ($34.8\%$) include two or more stems, supporting
source separation, automatic mixing, and stem-conditioned synthesis.
The corpus also enables MIDI-to-audio, transcription, and symbolic--audio
modeling.
Matched-step separation and transcription experiments confirm in-domain
learnability, with separation complementary to Slakh2100.
We release SPDMX with PDMX-aligned metadata and an open-source rendering
pipeline, so the same recipe can be applied to other symbolic corpora when
upstream scores permit redistributable audio synthesis.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
Public-domain music, audio datasets, multitrack stems, symbolic-to-audio,
source separation
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Massive public datasets have enabled significant progress in machine
learning (ML) over the past decade, driven by the insight that simply
scaling training data can yield substantial performance gains. Numerous
domains have benefited from these massive corpora: text with
C4~\cite{raffel2020exploring} and The Pile~\cite{gao2020pile}, images with
ImageNet~\cite{deng2009imagenet} and LAION-5B~\cite{schuhmann2022laion},
and even general audio and speech with AudioSet~\cite{gemmeke2017audio} and
LibriSpeech~\cite{panayotov2015librispeech}. Music cannot afford that same
luxury. Despite vast quantities of music abounding on the internet,
copyright licenses block public access to these potential large datasets.
Meanwhile, modern audio-domain music models require increasingly large
quantities of data, with a growing number of tasks requirining
\emph{individual stems}, such as source separation, accompaniment
generation, automatic mixing, and stem-conditioned synthesis. Therein lies
a central issue to modern AI-Music research: models require large
multitrack music corpora, but legally redistributable datasets for training
these systems remain scarce.

While multitrack music resources exist, they trade off three key
components: realism, scale, and redistributability. Recorded datasets such
as MUSDB18~\cite{rafii2019musdb18},
MedleyDB~\cite{bittner2014medleydb,bittner2016medleydb}, and
MoisesDB~\cite{pereira2023moisesdb} provide realistic, high-quality
stem-separated data, but suffer from small corpora sizes and restricted
access. Slakh2100~\cite{manilow2019cutting} remedies that scale issue by
synthesizing abundant MIDI data as multitrack audio but does so with
non-redistributable commercial instruments; furthermore, despite its
impressive scale, the size of Slakh2100 still pales in comparison to
existing symbolic music collections. No existing resource combines all
three goals of realism, scale, and redistributability.

Symbolic music datasets, namely Public Domain MusicXML
(PDMX)~\cite{long2024pdmx}, offer a path to scale and legal redistribution.
PDMX collects public domain symbolic music from MuseScore into a corpus of
$\sim$254K largely classical and folk songs. While we could simply render
with a General MIDI (GM) soundfont, previous
work~\cite{manilow2019cutting} has shown this insufficient: GM soundfonts
often have dry timbres and poor sustain for winds, brass, and strings. Such
naive rendering would lack the realism outlined earlier as a central
component of music datasets.

To alleviate this issue, we introduce SPDMX, a listening-guided rendering of
PDMX that pairs public-domain scores with redistributable stems and mixtures
at unprecedented scale. SPDMX comprises 5{,}469 song hours, around half from
multitrack ($\geq 2$ stems) scores. Even so, this license-free audio remains
useful in the single-track setting for generation, transcription, and
analysis. Our rendering chain is entirely open source: we clean PDMX's MIDI
files, then use listener preference to render stems with a diverse,
open-source soundfont collection or MIDI-DDSP~\cite{wu2022midi} when
possible. SPDMX is meant for use with the original PDMX dataset via shared
metadata. To our knowledge, SPDMX is the largest audio-domain music
dataset, whose public-domain licensing allows free, unrestricted use.

SPDMX combines a fully open synthesis protocol, listener-selected rendering
strategies, and task-based evaluations: we align a symbolic and audio corpus
at scale in the public domain, select category-specific hybrid
FluidSynth/MIDI-DDSP recipes through a perceptual study, and run
matched-budget source-separation and transcription evaluations on synthetic
and recorded test domains.
Upon acceptance, we will make SPDMX available through our demo
page\footnote{\url{\demourl}}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:prior}

Recorded multitrack datasets provide realistic supervision for music
generation and source separation but remain too small and/or restricted for
large-scale open training. MUSDB18~\cite{rafii2019musdb18},
MedleyDB~\cite{bittner2014medleydb,bittner2016medleydb}, and
MoisesDB~\cite{pereira2023moisesdb}
all comprise high-quality multitrack studio recordings, and they
consistently find use in training source-separation and generative models.
However, their smaller sizes (covering $\sim$10, $\sim$13.8, and $\sim$14.4
song-hours, respectively) and restriction to academic research settings
limit their use for large-scale training that has proven so successful in
other ML domains.

Slakh2100~\cite{manilow2019cutting} set a precedent for using synthetic
datasets derived from symbolic music---in its case, Lakh
MIDI~\cite{raffel2016learning}---to increase scale. While its quality lags
behind that of actual studio-recorded datasets, Slakh2100 remains a popular
choice for its large collection of $\sim$145 song-hours of multitrack
music. However, Slakh2100's rendering process depends on proprietary sample
libraries that prevent the synthesis of similar large symbolic music
corpora as multitrack audio. SPDMX offers an alternative open-source
paradigm for rendering coherent stems and mixtures from symbolic music:
public-domain scores, open renderers, much greater scale, and end-to-end
redistributability.

Neural MIDI synthesis offers a path to improving realism for specific
instruments, which motivates a hybrid rendering pipeline instead of one
entirely reliant on sample libraries. MIDI-DDSP~\cite{wu2022midi} has
proven well-suited for rendering MIDI datasets as realistic
audio~\cite{wu2022chamber}. However, despite strong results, its usefulness
is limited to its training data, the URMP~\cite{li2018creating} dataset,
which means that we can only use MIDI-DDSP for select orchestral
instruments. Hence, we can render with MIDI-DDSP on instruments when it
yields more realistic stems, which we can decide through a listening study,
and fall back to sample libraries for the rest.

\section{SPDMX Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

SPDMX has $253{,}982$ songs ($5{,}469$ song-hours) and $490{,}159$ mono
FLAC stems, plus paired MIDI. Song-hours are mixture durations counted once
per song (not multiplied by stem count). Of these, $88{,}403$ songs
($34.8\%$; $2{,}734$ song-hours, $50.0\%$ of hours) have $\geq 2$ stems
(mean $1.93$ stems per song, max $66$). Fig.~\ref{fig:stems_per_song}
compares multitrack song-hours by stem count against Slakh2100: even
excluding SPDMX's $165{,}579$ singles ($65.2\%$), the multitrack subset
alone already exceeds Slakh2100's full $145$\,h inventory. A BDGP-eligible
subset (bass, drums, guitar, and piano present) supports matched Slakh2100
comparisons. Songs share identity with PDMX (e.g., MusicXML, metadata) and
use only open synthesizers, keeping SPDMX open-source and license-free.

We build the release in three stages: clean PDMX scores, determine the most
realistic renderer for each instrument category, and assemble the full
release from those stems.

\subsection{Initial data cleaning}
\label{ssec:cleaning}

PDMX MIDI programs often disagree with track names (e.g., a named violin
encoded as piano). We correct GM programs from track name aliases and drop
tracks with no note events. Fig.~\ref{fig:gm_programs} shows the corrected
inventory by stem count and non-silent hours: for each rendered stem, we
sum $0.25$\,s windows whose RMS amplitude is at least $0.01$
($\approx -40$\,dBFS), excluding near-silent gaps between notes and
trailing padding. Much of SPDMX concentrates in piano, voice, drums, and
common orchestral families. Piano dominates stem count ($57\%$) more than
non-silent hours ($41\%$), indicating that many scores may include a piano
track, but this track is often sparse or short.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/gm_program_counts_compare.pdf}
\caption{SPDMX stem counts and non-silent hours by listening category (same
ten labels used in the rendering study). Categories marked $*$ are
MIDI-DDSP eligible in the frozen hybrid recipe (strings, wind, brass).}
\label{fig:gm_programs}
\end{figure}

Although cleaning makes the symbolic source of SPDMX more reliable, it does
not resolve the acoustic limitations of using a single GM bank to render
the whole dataset. Naive GM rendering remains dry and synthetic, and short
soundfont notes poorly match acoustic sustain for winds, brass, and
strings. That motivates listening among open FluidSynth banks and selective
MIDI-DDSP.

\subsection{Listening study}
\label{ssec:listening}

We use a listening study to determine a render recipe for each instrument
category. Every track becomes a mono stem at $44.1$\,kHz.
The \emph{basic} soundfont strategy uses one GM bank (\texttt{SGM-V2.01}).
The \emph{varied} strategy samples from a shortlist of redistributable
soundfonts per category, including \texttt{FluidR3\_GM},
\texttt{GeneralUser GS}, \texttt{Arachno}, and \texttt{Airfont}, plus a
light reverb FX profile, for Slakh2100-like timbral variety without
Slakh2100's proprietary libraries. For eligible monophonic strings, brass, and winds,
MIDI-DDSP~\cite{wu2022midi} can replace the soundfont; however, we fall
back to FluidSynth for polyphonic parts, instruments outside the MIDI-DDSP
(URMP) inventory, and all other GM programs.
Voice-class tracks use choir or voice soundfonts (lyric singing synthesis is
out of scope).

We ask listeners to compare four families on perceived realism: basic,
varied, MIDI-DDSP over basic fallbacks, and MIDI-DDSP over varied
fallbacks. The open reference is the basic soundfont, which also appears as
a hidden reference among shuffled unlabeled conditions. Each trial is a
$10$-second stem excerpt scored for perceived realism ($0$--$100$). For
each of the 10 categories, the study has two stems. We had $N{=}8$
listeners complete the study (Fig.~\ref{fig:listening}).

Based on the results of the listening study, we develop a ``recipe'' for
rendering SPDMX: for each category, we pick one of the four families as
winner, choosing the highest mean realism by default. When basic only
narrowly beats the varied family, we keep varied for diversity. SPDMX uses
basic for drums, organ, and guitar, and varied for piano, voice, mallet,
and residual miscellaneous classes; strings and brass use MIDI-DDSP over
basic, and wind uses MIDI-DDSP over varied ($\star$ in
Fig.~\ref{fig:listening}).

\subsection{Final assembly}
\label{ssec:assembly}

The frozen recipe is applied to every cleaned song under the previously
mentioned redistributable synthesizers. Because independent per-stem
loudness matching would erase MIDI velocity balance, we loudness-normalize
each stem toward $-23$\,LUFS~\cite{series2011algorithms} with peak
limiting, after which we scale each stem by the ratio of that track's
maximum note-on velocity to the song maximum. We uniformly-apply a single
anti-clip peak gain on the stem sum in order to keeps stems linearly
summable (mixture equals stem sum)~\cite{manilow2019cutting}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate whether SPDMX provides learnable supervision for source
separation and multi-instrument transcription. These runs validate the
release rather than claim state-of-the-art performance. To limit
confounding factors such as training duration and dataset size, we match
the training budget of every training arm within each task.

\subsection{Source separation}
\label{ssec:separation}

We fine-tune four-source Hybrid Demucs~\cite{defossez2021hybrid} on
\emph{bass}, \emph{drums}, \emph{guitar}, and \emph{piano} (BDGP) mixtures
using the Slakh2100 four-stem protocol. SPDMX stems map from General MIDI
program ranges, Slakh2100 stems from dataset metadata; we keep only songs
that contain all four targets (the BDGP-eligible SPDMX pool described in
\S\ref{ssec:assembly}). We train on three arms that share the same step
budget on $10$\,s crops (mono duplicated to stereo): Slakh2100 alone, the
SPDMX pool alone, and their union. We report scale-invariant
SI-SDR~\cite{leroux2019sdr} on held-out Slakh2100 and SPDMX
mixtures, and on recorded corpora only where a target maps
cleanly: MUSDB18~\cite{rafii2019musdb18} (bass and drums),
MedleyDB~\cite{bittner2014medleydb,bittner2016medleydb} (instrument tags),
and MoisesDB~\cite{pereira2023moisesdb} (native stem names).

in-domain BD scores on both synthetic sets and slightly improves recorded
BD, yet it does not close the pitched-stem gap. Taken together, open
rendering and PDMX-scale inventory yield learnable synthetic supervision,
but they do not by themselves transfer pitched stems to real multitrack
recordings.

\subsection{Transcription}
\label{ssec:transcription}

Because SPDMX pairs mixtures with MIDI, we also ask whether it
supports multi-instrument automatic music
transcription~\cite{gardner2022mt3,chang2024yourmt3}. We train a
YourMT3-style encoder--decoder (T5-small, trained from scratch) for
$100$K matched steps on mixture audio with aligned MIDI, comparing
Slakh2100-redux against SPDMX songs with at least two stems. We evaluate
onset F1 (pitch and onset time, without requiring correct offsets) and
multi-instrument note F1 (the same criterion with an instrument label
match) on Slakh-test and a held-out SPDMX split. This setup is
intentionally small in training budget and omits the
pretrained recipes used in published MT3-style systems, so absolute scores
remain low; longer training would likely improve them. Still, we can
already observe a few trends (Table~\ref{tab:transcription}).

On Slakh-test, Slakh- and SPDMX-trained models reach similar onset F1
($\approx 0.07$), so additional SPDMX hours do not improve transfer under
this budget. On held-out SPDMX, onset F1 rises to $0.27$, with a similar
gain in offset-aware metrics, suggesting the aligned audio and MIDI are
learnable in domain. Instrument-wise scores favor drums under Slakh
training, while SPDMX in-domain performance is strongest on piano (then
strings) and remains weak on bass and guitar, reflecting differences in
instrument mix and rendering between the two corpora.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

SPDMX renders PDMX under a listening-guided open pipeline into what is, to
our knowledge, the largest license-free music audio dataset: $253{,}982$
songs, $490{,}159$ mono stems, and $5{,}469$ song-hours at $44.1$\,kHz,
with aligned PDMX metadata and MIDI throughout.
$88{,}403$ songs ($34.8\%$) include two or more stems for multitrack
supervision.
As a whole, the release supports separation, automatic mixing,
stem-conditioned generation, and MIDI-to-audio research without copyrighted
recordings or proprietary sample libraries.
Matched-step experiments show that SPDMX is learnable for separation in
domain, especially for bass and drums, and can be trained jointly with
Slakh2100, while transfer of pitched stems to recorded multitracks remains
an open challenge. Under a short training budget, transcription likewise
learns from SPDMX's aligned mixtures and MIDI in domain, suggesting the
release already supports multi-instrument AMT research.
Because the render stack is fully open source and redistributable, the same
recipe can be extended synthesize other symbolic corpora.
We hope SPDMX finds use as training and evaluation data for source
separation, stem-conditioned generation and editing, automatic mixing,
multitrack and MIDI-aligned transcription, accompaniment and arrangement
models, and joint symbolic--audio research.

\section{Compliance with Ethical Standards}
\label{sec:ethics}

SPDMX is derived from public-domain PDMX and released for open research
redistribution.
Rendering uses redistributable soundfonts and open MIDI-DDSP weights, not
closed commercial VST sample libraries.
